# 6. Summary of Insights

Based on the full exploratory analysis—including numeric trends, categorical distributions, statistical testing, and detailed plot interpretations—the following major insights emerge:

### Academic Pressure is the strongest predictor among numeric variables
- Shows the highest correlation with depression (+0.47) among all numeric features.
- Depressed students report higher academic pressure on average.
- Both t-tests and Mann-Whitney tests confirm the difference is highly statistically significant.
- This suggests academic strain is a central driver of mental health challenges in this dataset.

### Study Satisfaction has one of the clearest behavioral separations
- Strong negative correlation with depression (–0.17).
- Depressed students show consistently lower satisfaction with their studies.
- Histogram and boxplot patterns highlight distinct group trends.
- Supports the idea that subjective academic well-being is more informative than actual grades (CGPA).

### Suicidal Thoughts are the largest categorical differentiator
- Students who have had suicidal thoughts are overwhelmingly more likely to be classified as depressed.
- 85%+ of depressed students report suicidal thoughts.
- Only a small minority of non-depressed students report past suicidal ideation.
- This variable behaves almost like a proxy indicator for severe depression.

### Sleep Duration and Dietary Habits show meaningful lifestyle effects
- Short sleep (<5 hours) appears much more common among depressed students.
- Unhealthy diets are significantly more prevalent among depressed individuals.
- These variables point toward behavioral and lifestyle patterns that align with mental health deterioration.

### Work-Study Hours show a moderate association with depression
- Slight positive correlation (+0.21): depressed students tend to work/study more hours.
- This may reflect overwork, poor time balance, or external pressures.

### CGPA does not meaningfully differentiate depressed vs non-depressed students
- Nearly identical distributions across groups.
- Suggests academic performance itself is not the issue, but rather the pressure and satisfaction surrounding academics.

### Age shows a mild inverse relationship with depression
- Younger students are more likely to be depressed.
- Could reflect academic transitions, early stress, or lack of coping mechanisms.

### Family History and Financial Stress are also meaningful contributors
- Higher financial stress levels strongly align with depression.
- Students with a family history of mental illness show higher depression rates.
- These variables reinforce the idea of both environmental and genetic susceptibility.

## Overall Conclusion

The EDA indicates that depression in students is shaped far more by stress, satisfaction, lifestyle, and psychological indicators than by academic performance itself.

The strongest drivers are:
- High academic pressure
- Low study satisfaction
- Suicidal ideation
- Short sleep duration
- Unhealthy diet
- High work-study hours
- Financial stress

This suggests future modeling efforts should prioritize stress-related, behavioral, and psychological variables, as they show the clearest and most consistent relationships with depression.


### Data Quality
- No missing values detected across any variables, reducing the need for imputation and ensuring model integrity.
- No duplicate records, confirming clean data collection and preventing artificial bias.
- Consistent data types (numeric, categorical, ordinal) with no format anomalies.
- Balanced feature ranges aside from a few naturally skewed stress-related variables.
- Survey-based dataset, so responses may include subjective bias but remain interpretable and structured.
- Overall conclusion: Dataset is clean, reliable, and ready for EDA and modeling with minimal preprocessing.

### Major Depression Indicators
- Academic Pressure: Students reporting high academic pressure showed noticeably higher depression probability.
- Financial Stress: One of the strongest correlates with depression—students under financial strain consistently scored higher on depression risk.
- Suicidal Thoughts: Clear and direct association with the highest predicted depression likelihood.
- Study Satisfaction: Low satisfaction is a strong negative indicator and clusters strongly in the high-risk group.
- Sleep Duration: Students sleeping < 5 hours show significantly elevated depression risk, with a sharp jump compared to 6–8 hours.
- Work Hours: Moderate evidence that students working longer hours (> 20 hours/week) show higher stress, indirectly supporting depression risk.
- CGPA: Lower CGPA shows a weak–moderate negative correlation with well-being but is not as strong as stress-related variables.

### Modeling Considerations
- Class Imbalance present (58.5% non-depressed vs 41.5% depressed)
  - Use techniques such as SMOTE, class weights, or undersampling.
- Normalization needed for numerical variables (e.g., sleep hours, CGPA, weekly work hours) to improve model convergence.
- Categorical Variable Encoding:
   - Ordinal (e.g., study satisfaction, academic pressure) → Ordinal encoding
   - Nominal (e.g., gender, family history categories) → One-hot encoding
Feature Engineering Opportunities:
- Sleep × Academic Pressure → captures interaction between overwork and exhaustion
- Study Satisfaction × CGPA → academic performance + perception of workload
- Work Hours × Financial Stress → combined economic strain
- Sleep Regularity (binary: <5 hours vs ≥5 hours)
- High-risk composite score combining pressure, stress, sleep, and satisfaction
Potential Model Types:
- Baseline: Logistic Regression
- Tree-based: Random Forest, XGBoost
- Probability-based: Naive Bayes
Model Evaluation:
- Precision/Recall (important due to imbalance)
- ROC-AUC
- Calibration curves if predicting risk probabilities
